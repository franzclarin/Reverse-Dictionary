Mine hard negatives from observed retrieval failures

P3 · training data
In plain terms

Show the model harder wrong answers during training — but not the ones it is usually told to use.

Today — training mostly shows the model obviously-unrelated wrong answers, which are too easy to reject and so teach it little.

Do — feed it genuine near-misses instead: the words the live system actually returns when it gets a question wrong.

Why it matters — filed at low priority on purpose. The problem this technique is usually aimed at — confusing close synonyms — is worth at most 3.4 points here, and this project deliberately scores a synonym as a correct answer. Training the model to pull synonyms apart would push against its own scoring rule.

Depends on: RD-14 — negatives are mined against queries, and there are none yet Feeds: RD-09 Touches: a dev-only mining script in scripts/, reusing scripts/lib/retrieval.ts Risk: optimising strict R@1 at the cost of lenient R@1 — the metric the decision rule actually resolves on Est: S once RD-14's queries exist; the mining itself is a top-k scan the harness already performs

Contrastive training with in-batch negatives learns to separate a target from whatever happened to share its batch, which is usually easy. Mining confusable negatives is a real and standard improvement. Filed here at low priority with its stated motivation corrected, because the specific problem it is usually proposed to fix is measured at 3.4 points on this system, and the obvious way to mine them can cost more than that.

The synonym-collapse framing does not survive contact with the metrics

"Synonym collapse" is worth at most 3.4 points here: strict R@1 is 20.6% and lenient R@1 is 24.0%, and the entire difference is queries where the system returned a word this project has already ruled acceptable. Lenient R@1 is the metric the decision rule resolves on (METHODS §9a). So training the encoder to push near-synonyms apart optimises a 3.4pp strict gap while actively risking the headline number — the eval set's acceptable[] field exists precisely because returning a synonym is not an error. Mine negatives, but not from a synonym list.

Blocked by its own dependency — RD-16, 2026-08-28

This ticket mines negatives against RD-14's synthetic queries, and RD-14 is now held: RD-16 measured its premise on the live index and found the register gap at +1.8pp, down from +26.1pp. There are no queries to mine against, and the retrain they would feed (RD-09) is held on its own evidence — a same-size encoder trained on 215M QA pairs scored 7.0 points below the fine-tune.

Nothing here is wrong; it is simply third in a chain whose first two links are now held. The task item that survives independently is the margin probe — this ticket already requires re-running scripts/probe-margins.ts on the gloss index before assuming the remaining failures have the shape hard negatives address, and RD-16 did not do that. It remains the honest first step if this is ever picked up.

Task

  1. Mine from measured failures, not from a synonym heuristic. The per-query results arrays in eval/runs/*.json are a direct record of what the live system actually returns instead of the right answer — the empirically confusable words, not the ones a thesaurus predicts. Use those as the negative source. Same mechanism as top-k mining, aimed at observed errors rather than assumed ones.
  2. Never mine using the eval set's own queries. Negatives mined from v1.jsonl queries and then trained on are training on the test set, and every subsequent number on that set becomes meaningless. Mine over RD-14's synthetic queries or a held-out slice only. This is the reason this ticket depends on RD-14 rather than being independent of it.
  3. Exclude synset mates and acceptable[] members from the negative pool. A word sharing the target's synset is by construction not a wrong answer — synset mates share one gloss and their vectors are bit-identical, so labelling one a negative asks the model to separate two copies of the same text. Filter them out explicitly rather than trusting the mining to avoid them.
  4. Watch echo as the primary regression. Echo fell 40.7% → 14.5% at the cutover and CLAUDE.md treats it as a headline metric. Negatives drawn from the same semantic field are drawn from exactly the neighbourhood where echo lives; a mining strategy that raises it back has traded the cutover's main win for a smaller one.
  5. Report strict and lenient R@1 separately in any evaluation of the result, for the reason in the callout. A run that shows strict up and lenient flat has demonstrated the failure mode, not a win.
  6. Consider whether this belongs to the reranker instead. Hard negatives are the standard way to train a cross-encoder, and RD-12's reranker addresses 53 points of headroom against this path's 23. If RD-12 fine-tunes its cross-encoder, this mining work likely has a better home there — reassess before doing it for the bi-encoder.

Prerequisite knowledge

What the ranking failures actually are

CLAUDE.md's margin analysis found that on the lemma index, echo results outscored the true target by +0.134 mean cosine and non-echo results by +0.094 — the target sat below nearly everything. That was a representation failure, not a negatives failure, and it is what RD-02 fixed by changing what was indexed. Re-run the margin probe (scripts/probe-margins.ts) on the gloss index before assuming the remaining failures have the shape hard negatives address.

Synset ties are not a defect

The synset-keyed collapse was measured lossless: 287/287 identical top-10 order, 0 discordant rank-1 pairs, 204,549 gloss rows collapsing to 114,662 with 0 divergent. Where two synonyms tie, the tie-break is --expansion-order wordnet — sense familiarity as a prior — and CLAUDE.md already flags that choosing it on benchmark score alone is mild benchmark-fitting. Ties are handled; they are not the thing to train away.

Learnings

!

"Fix synonym collapse" assumes synonyms are errors; here they are scored as correct. This project chose lenient R@1 as its decision metric and built acceptable[] to support it, so an intervention that separates synonyms is optimising against its own scoring rule. A technique that is right in general can be aimed at the wrong target in a specific system — check which metric moves before adopting the framing that comes with the technique.

✓

The system's own errors are a better negative source than a lexical resource. WordNet can say which words are related; only the run artifacts can say which words this encoder actually confuses. The second set is smaller, more specific, and already sitting in eval/runs/.

Acceptance criteria

  • Negatives mined from observed retrieval failures, reusing scripts/lib/retrieval.ts, not a second search path
  • No negatives mined using eval/sets/v1.jsonl queries
  • Synset mates and acceptable[] members explicitly excluded from the negative pool
  • Margin probe re-run on the gloss index before assuming the failure shape
  • Any resulting model reported with strict and lenient R@1, plus echo, paired vs. the current production run
  • Explicit decision recorded on whether the negatives serve the bi-encoder (RD-09) or RD-12's cross-encoder
